They loved the demo. Then the thread went quiet.

We called it timing. The data says otherwise.

Ultha Moktan · Nov 16, 2026 · 6 min read

A great demo used to feel like the hard part was over. Someone said they loved it. You sent the recap. The deal was supposed to move.

That data comes from Matt Dixon and Ted McKenna, authors of The JOLT Effect, who studied more than 2.5 million recorded sales conversations. In Harvard Business Review they wrote that "anywhere between 40% and 60% of deals today end up lost to customers who express their intent to purchase, but ultimately fail to act."

I probably don't need to tell you how that silence feels.

If you sell AI, the buyer already has another way to do the job. A spreadsheet. A junior hire. Or a prompt they already run.

Today I'm unpacking 7 reasons a great demo still turns into "not right now."

What stood out to me the most: how easy it is to mistake praise for a real plan to buy. And how often people who meant to buy still never do.

1. "This is awesome" doesn't mean they'll buy.

Can you spot the line that does?

Only the last one is a buying signal. It names a project with a deadline: cutting the ticket backlog before Q1. It also asks how this fits Zendesk, a tool they already use. The other three are compliments.

Praise on a good call is easy to treat as a buying signal. That is what Rob Snyder, who wrote The Power of Pull, summed up well: it is "so simple to mistake 'enthusiasm' for 'demand' in a sales call."

You will hear that the demo was awesome, that it resonates, or that the company would definitely benefit. Those lines sound like demand. They usually are not. What Rob looks for is whether the buyer is fitting this into a project they are personally trying to get done right now. That can sound like they are already trying to solve this, or like a question about how you fit a system they already use. It does not sound like a what-if about a big company copying you.

My rule of thumb: write down the project they named after the demo. If you only have compliments, you heard enthusiasm.

2. Up to 60% of deals with buying intent end in no decision.

Pushing harder backfired 84% of the time.

I used to think a quiet thread after a great call meant they needed more time. Not so.

Matt and Ted share that same range on their JOLT Effect site. They say those deals were lost to customer indecision, not to a competing vendor. 44% of those no-decision losses came from a preference for the status quo. The other 56% came from risk or fear of failure.

Sellers who pushed harder against the status quo found that "this approach backfired 84% of the time." I suspect a lot of the quiet threads after a great call sit in that second group. The buyer meant it. Then the risk of being wrong took over. A lot of those still get logged as timing.

3. Your biggest competitor is a spreadsheet.

It's cheap, easy and works well enough.

What they already use can beat a strong demo.

To name the real competition, April Dunford tells teams to think about competitive alternatives, not only rival products. The first alternative to beat is the status quo: how they were solving it today. Buyers use spreadsheets, documents, and manual processes. Or they make do with a limited feature inside a bigger tool they already have. Those options, she writes, "are chosen because they are easy to get started with, cheap or free, and frequently do the job good enough in a pinch." Sometimes they would do nothing. Sometimes they would hire an intern to handle it.

Ask how they handle this today. Then ask whether that still works well enough for them.

4. They wanted it. They stayed put anyway.

Switching felt scarier than staying.

A buyer can want the product and still stay put.

In Jobs-to-be-Done interviewing, Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek developed the Four Forces of Progress. On jobstobedone.org, a customer switches when the pain of today plus the pull of a better life are stronger than fear and habit. Push is the pain of how things work now. Pull is the better life they can picture. Anxiety is fear that it will not work, that they will look foolish, or that something will happen to their data. Habit is sticking with what they already know and do. Most teams spend their energy making the product more attractive. They skip how scary the switch feels, and how easy it is to stay put. Bob and Chris write, "Flip that inequality and the customer stays put, no matter how good your product is."

Where I'd bet: most early-stage AI pitches lean hard on pull. They skip the messy switch the buyer is already picturing.

5. They filed you under "another chatbot."

So they guessed the wrong price and the wrong buyer.

The category you pick tells the buyer what to assume. Call something a CRM and buyers assume you compete with Salesforce. They assume you sell to the head of sales. They assume you cost less than the market leader. An AI buyer who files you as another chatbot will guess the wrong user, the wrong features, and the wrong price. The same thing happens if they see you as a feature of a tool they already have.

Here is how April Dunford put it: positioning "sets off a really powerful set of assumptions about who your product competes with, what features your product should have, who the product is intended for, and even things like what the product should cost." Good positioning sets off assumptions that are true. Bad positioning sets off assumptions that are not.

6. Your champion asked for budget and got shut down.

The person who loved your demo still needs a CFO's yes.

The person who loved the demo may still get shut down in finance.

This one is also from Rob, in a demand-audit essay. He describes a friend's startup that found demand, then lost promising deals when champions asked their CFOs for budget and got shut down. He asks whether that is a demand problem. Then he answers: "Probably not - it seems like a 'we need to help our champion not get wrecked by their CFO' problem."

Rob is describing one pattern from a demand audit. My hunch is that the person who loved your demo often walks into finance without a project the company already funded. Ask what their finance person will want to know. Then send a short note they can forward.

7. They paid, never used it, then left.

Elena Verna calls them "sleeping bears."

Paying customers can look fine while they never log in. In a January 13, 2026 post, Elena Verna warns against "'sleeping bears' - customers who pay but don't use the product, and wake up just in time to churn." She raises this while arguing for monetization that lines up with customer outcomes. Look at who is paying and not using the product now, before they churn.

What to steal

Three things I would take from this list:

  1. After each demo this week, write one sentence about the project they said they were trying to finish. If you cannot name that project, you heard enthusiasm.
  2. Talk to someone who saw the demo and did not move forward. Happy customers will tell you why they bought. People who stalled or left will tell you what got in the way.
  3. Open three quiet deals and replace timing with a more specific reason, or write that you do not know yet. If you cannot name the reason, that is useful too. It tells you what to find out next.

Looking at these reasons, I have found the hardest part for founders is getting the real story after a quiet thread. Timing is the easy label. So I talk to a startup's customers, starting with the ones who use it most. The point is to hear what they would not say to the founder.
